# Engineering standards

## Privacy invariants

- Never write camera frames, face crops, microphone PCM, names, device identifiers, or biometric features to storage or logs.
- Camera input must be reduced in-memory to low-resolution motion components before it enters the world model.
- Actual visitor audio is authorized in RAM only. SessionAudio owns bounded PCM, separate from serializable WhisperMemory; never persist, export, or log it. Clear it on reset, microphone shutdown, and page close. Suppression is unconditional for actual recording, including when audioRecursion is nonzero.
- Any future sensing feature needs a regression test that proves its persisted representation cannot contain raw media.
- Anonymous debug recordings are one-second samples in a 3,600-event ring. Keep the compact tuple schema and validate imports before replay; coordinates are data, never instructions.
- Speaker playback must extend the microphone suppression window and cannot shorten an existing hold. Real voice capture never weakens suppression; the legacy audioRecursion setting has no effect.

## Memory invariants

- Nothing recalled remains unchanged. Every ghost and whisper recall must increase `recallCount`, reduce `fidelity`, mutate its representation, and reinforce bounded `strength`.
- Composite false memories retain the IDs of every source path and whisper in `lineage`. Provenance is internal and must not become visitor-facing metadata.
- Camera points reach every projected immediate trace, path, and field deposit through the same four-point homography. Never calibrate only the visible renderer while leaving the world model in camera coordinates.
- Occluded tracks remain predicted but do not deposit dwell memory. Expire them after four seconds unless a new detection re-associates with the predicted position.
- Occluded tracks are association state, not observed motion. Keep them out of immediate rendering as well as memory deposition.

## Observable behaviour

- Behaviours are derived from anonymous motion only: centroids, velocities, the size of the detected region, and how those relate between people over time. No pose model, no face, no identity.
- **Two ideas were assessed and cannot be built this way.** *Looking toward the camera* needs a face or gaze model — motion sees where someone is, never where they are pointed. *Walking backwards* needs orientation; without knowing which way a body faces, retreating and reversing are the same signal, and `revisit` covers the honest half of it. Do not ship an approximation of either and call it the real thing; the effect depends entirely on the visitor believing the room.
- Every detector needs a minimum duration, a cooldown, and thresholds chosen so ordinary milling about does not fire it. **A behaviour announced wrongly is worse than one missed** — one wrong call and the visitor stops believing any of them.
- Each detector ships with its negative case under test: side-by-side walking is not following, standing still is not waving, people already together are not approaching, opposite headings are not mirroring. A detector without its negative is not tested.
- Waving is detectable because a still centroid with a changing extent means arms. That is why `VisitorTrack` carries `extent`.
- **A line may claim only what the detector measured.** The wave detector knows a still centroid whose outline keeps changing; it does not know a hand did it, because a coat opening or a broad stretch produces the same signal. `room-voice.test.ts` rejects lines naming body parts or acts of perception the sensing cannot support. This is the same rule as "a behaviour announced wrongly is worse than one missed", applied to the words rather than the detection — and it was broken first by this codebase, not by a hypothetical future one.
- Every `BehaviorKind` must have lines in `VOICE_LINES`. Noticing something and having nothing to say about it is the same silence as not noticing it.

## Bounds

- **"Bounded in practice" is not bounded.** A cap enforced by a time window, by how fast a caller happens to step its clock, or by how often a branch happens to be taken, is a cap that holds until something changes. Every accumulating structure carries its ceiling: `SAMPLES_MAX` on a behaviour window, `LINEAGE_MAX` on a composite's provenance, `MEMORY_ID_MAX` on an id.
- **Measured, not feared.** Composite false-memory ids used to embed both parents' ids, so a composite of two composites embedded theirs. The length roughly 2.6x'd per generation and reached **38,312 characters by the eighth**, written to disk on every save. A composite is now named *after* its parents — `false-<base36 time>-<FNV-1a of both>` — and the provenance lives in `lineage`, which is where the recall rules already put it and which is bounded too.
- **The Rust caps are the executable form of this.** `room_protocol` could not represent what the TypeScript was producing, which is how the id growth was found at all. When the two disagree about a bound, the disagreement is the finding; do not widen the Rust to accept what the TypeScript emits without asking why the TypeScript emits it.
- **One unreadable memory costs the room that memory, not the room.** Serde aborts a whole document on the first element it cannot read, so `PersistentState::parse` filters the collections before deserialising and reports how many it dropped. A gallery holding one over-long id would otherwise have opened to an empty room and lost weeks of history — and a room is not a file an operator can regenerate.

## The room's voice

- Speech is the **rarest** thing the installation does: a hard floor of one line per minute, and the room declines opportunities it is entitled to take. Most visitors get one line, many get none. A line that arrives often stops being part of the work and becomes a caption for it.
- **The room says "I" only when it is uncertain.** Observation is impersonal ("you stopped."); the first person appears only where the memory is unreliable ("i am no longer certain this happened."). `room-voice.test.ts` fails if an observational line reaches for "I".
- Lines are lowercase, end in a full stop, never exclaim or ask, and fit in 48 characters. A visitor reads them while walking.
- `RoomVoice` owns the decision to speak. Callers offer occasions freely and it usually refuses; no call site may be able to talk the room into narrating.
- A line lands just below its subject (`UTTERANCE_DROP`), never across it. On top of a vortex it is both illegible and a caption; underneath it stays attached to the person who caused it. Place it along the *route* of a detection, not at a track's last position — tracks end when their visitor leaves, so that position sits at the edge of the room and the line clamps into a corner.
- **Speech must close the microphone.** The room tone was kept below 150 Hz precisely so it could never be mistaken for a whisper; a spoken line sits squarely inside the 180–5000 Hz analysis band and cannot be separated that way. Without suppression the room records its own sentence as a visitor's whisper and replays it later as a memory of someone who was never there. Suppress on the call, not on the utterance's `start` event, which can fire after audio has already begun, and over-estimate the duration: a missed whisper is cheap, a self-recording is not.
- Platform subscriptions hand back their own unsubscribe rather than expecting a matching remove call later. `RoomSpeech` is rebuilt every time the microphone reconnects, which happens on a 2.5 s retry whenever a device drops, so a listener left behind accumulates one dead instance per reconnect over an unattended run. Returning the unsubscribe makes forgetting it a type error instead of a slow leak, and `room-speech.test.ts` runs 25 restarts and asserts nothing is left.
- Anything re-entrant on a retry path must retire what it is replacing first. The microphone restart now stops the previous audio engine and voice before constructing new ones, rather than trusting the disconnect handler to have done it.
- Only `localService` voices are used. Some platform voices synthesise on a server, which would make an offline installation quietly dependent on a connection. If no local voice exists the room stays silent rather than reaching for the network.
- The mechanism is borrowed from queue-line animatronics that fake their sensing entirely: a live image of you, statements true of every human, and timing. This room detects for real, so it can do the honest version — but only if the response lands within about a second of the act. Attribution is the whole point; a delayed answer is ambient animation.

## Randomness

- **The room has one stream, and it is a value rather than an ambient capability.** `RoomRng` in `lib/room-rng.ts`, seeded once per session, threaded into every probabilistic decision: whether to remember, which memory, how far a recalled path mutates, which whisper comes with it, where a whisper sits on the wall. A call to `Math.random()` in authoritative behaviour is a decision that cannot be replayed, and it is invisible — it produces a plausible room that no seed reproduces.
- `lib/room-rng.ts` and `crates/room-engine/src/rng.rs` are the same generator written twice, and `tests/vectors/rng.json` is the evidence. Regenerate it with `cargo run -p room-engine --example rng-vectors > tests/vectors/rng.json` and expect an empty diff; a non-empty one means every saved room resumes into a different stream.
- **sfc32 was chosen for reproducibility, not for quality.** Every operation is a 32-bit add, xor, shift or rotate, which JavaScript expresses exactly with `>>> 0` and `Math.imul`. PCG64 or xoshiro256 would need `BigInt` on the TypeScript side and a floating-point generator would not agree bit for bit at all — and two engines that cannot be given the same stream can only ever be compared with "these look similar".
- **A decision that cannot fire still draws.** `chance(0)` and `index([])` consume a value. Otherwise the stream depends on the operator's settings, and turning recall off in the console would silently change every unrelated decision the room makes afterwards.
- Restoring replays the draws rather than storing the generator's state words, so a saved room does not depend on the algorithm's internals. Seed plus position is the smaller, more durable claim; five million draws replay in well under a second, which is measured rather than assumed.
- **The renderer keeps its own randomness.** Where a particle starts and what the grain texture looks like are how a memory manifests, which is the renderer's decision. Only the room's cognition draws from the room's stream.

## Recall

- A recalled memory is rendered as a **body**, not as a marker: the same vortex, the same gathering shell, the same heat as a living visitor. A visitor alone in the room must not be able to tell what the room is remembering from who is actually there. Anything that labels a recall as a recall defeats the work.
- The single exception is `false`. A composite memory runs cold (`heat` 0.34 against 0.85–1.0 for genuine recalls), so it moves like a person and never warms. This is the only visitor-facing signal that a memory is fabricated, and it must stay wordless. `swarm.test.ts` fails if any other kind drops near it.
- Up to `MAX_ACTIVE_RECALLS` memories may run at once, and living visitors keep reserved body slots so recalls can never crowd them out. One-at-a-time made the room feel like it had a single thought.
- Recall durations live in `RECALL_PRESENTATION` and are bounded to 5–20 seconds. A recall that outlasts gallery dwell is one nobody ever sees complete.
- Rare-event rolls use `eventChance` against wall-clock time. Rolling once per analysis tick tied the rate to the hardware profile — the same installation remembered 2.4x more often on FULL (24 Hz) than on CHEAP (10 Hz). Advance the roll clock *before* any early return, or blocked time banks up and fires a burst when a slot frees.

## The particle field

- `swarm` is the default engine and the one the piece is designed around. `traces` is the fallback for machines without WebGL2 float render targets. Both engines support the subtractive `paper` surface. Switching surfaces must not silently replace the particle field with sparse traces: an empty trace scene looked like a blank white screen. Paper particles multiply absorption into the palette ground; room particles add light. Blackout always clears to black. Neither may regress: both are in the gate.
- The swarm's colour, budgets and dynamics live in `lib/swarm.ts`. The shader builds its gradient lookup from the same table the tests read, so the GPU and the tests cannot disagree about what the piece looks like.
- Colour maps how alive a place is: 0 is ground the room barely remembers, 1 is a body standing there now. **The ramp must run cold-to-hot and its 0 end must be chromatic.** An earlier version ran hot-to-cold, which meant an empty gallery — the state the piece is in most of its life — rendered as flat white static. Chroma floors on the stops did not catch this, because the stops were fine and the common case was not; `swarm.test.ts` now asserts the empty-room colour directly.
- No cyan stop. Cyan carries very little CIELAB chroma while being extremely luminous, so every version with one either failed the chroma floor or put a brightness spike early in the ramp, making barely-remembered ground glow brighter than a person.
- Particle budgets must pack into exact squares. Rounding up to a power of two silently promoted STANDARD's 147,456 particles to FULL's 262,144 and made the profile meaningless.
- Force terms must be scaled, not divided by their sample step. Dividing a finite difference by `2e` gave the memory term a force of ~40 against a body push of 7.5: the swarm collapsed onto a few filaments, most of the wall went empty, and a visitor standing anywhere else got no answer.
- **A body must add light, not remove it.** Pure repulsion is measurable causality and bad art — it punched a hole and dropped local brightness 62%. Bodies now repel inside the core and gather from the outer band, so a person reads as a bright vortex. Verified by measurement: local brightness rises ~49% where a body stirs.
- Body radius is deliberately small (0.14 of the room). At 0.26 a single visitor evacuated a quarter of the wall.

## Projected colour

- Every colour the installation puts on the wall is declared in `lib/palette.ts`. No draw call may contain a colour literal: the legibility floors in `palette.test.ts` can only hold if there is one place to hold.
- Each mark must clear ΔE 12 against its own ground, and the six recall events (`ghostRecall`, `emptyGhost`, `stillnessRecall`, `returnEcho`, `convergence`, `falseMemory`) must clear ΔE 12 against each other. Visitors are given no labels, so an event that is not chromatically distinct does not exist for them.
- Both projection modes are held to the same floors. `room` is the default because its ground demands under 1% of the projector's full-white output against `paper`'s 85%, and that flood is light the camera then has to reject — a sensor-stability property, not only an aesthetic one.
- Measured and rejected: the original palette drew graphite marks on `#eeede8` at alphas of 0.025–0.12. Every mark resolved between ΔE 1.5 and ΔE 6.9 of the ground, and `ghostRecall` against `falseMemory` was ΔE 0.80 — below a just-noticeable difference. Four of the six recall events were the same mark. Do not reintroduce low-alpha marks on the assumption that faintness reads as subtlety on a projector; it reads as absence.
- Also rejected, and the reason `paper` still exists: subtractive polarity is *not* physically unusable. Once its inks are strong enough it clears the same contrast bar under gallery ambient. The original design failed on contrast, not on polarity. Keep both modes honest rather than repeating the stronger claim.
- Anything drawn every frame into the accumulation buffer must be scaled by `depositPerFrame`. A buffer keeping `r` per frame and receiving `d` settles at `d / (1 - r)`, so depositing a mark's full single-pass alpha drives every channel to white within a second and destroys exactly the hue separation the floors above are protecting. The first build of the accumulating renderer did this; the screenshot showed a white rectangle where a palette was supposed to be.
- Standing presence accumulates in the retina; recall events are painted straight to the wall. Never re-deposit stored history (ghost trails, montages) into the retina — the same minutes get laid down on every frame and clip the image.
- Every `RoomPersonality` must have an entry in `PERSONALITY_RENDER` and `PERSONALITY_TONE`, and no two may be identical. The world model has always chosen between eight states; before these tables, six of them projected the same image and all eight sounded the same.

## Sound

- **The whisper threshold is calibrated to the room, not fixed.** Rooms differ by orders of magnitude: measured on a real session the noise floor was 0.0001 and speech peaked at 0.042, while the threshold sat at 0.035 — 84% of the loudest moment. Two samples in 307 crossed it and 161 seconds of talking produced one whisper. The configured value is a floor; `effectiveWhisperThreshold` lifts it above whatever this room actually sounds like. The floor tracker is asymmetric so a person talking cannot drag it up behind them.
- **Set level in one place — and check every place, not just the one that was reported.** After fixing a double-scaled replay buffer, the identical mistake was still present at capture: `finishWhisper` normalised the envelope against a hardcoded 0.18 chosen when the threshold was 0.035. Once quiet speech could be captured, every whisper was stored at a fraction of full scale and came back 12–15 dB too quiet. The envelope records a sound's *shape*; the gain node owns its loudness.
- Fixed formants on noise were rejected after 19 replay attempts still sounded like hiss. Real voices require actual microphone PCM. Replay session recordings at their original speed; old envelope-only memories cannot be reconstructed and must remain silent. Capture uses an AudioWorklet with 250 ms pre-roll and 400 ms silence allowance, bounded to 6.5 seconds per clip, 80 clips and 32 MiB total. No synthetic phrases substitute for visitors.
- Read a stored envelope by interpolation. At most 32 points stretched over seconds, nearest-neighbour indexing steps the amplitude audibly and adds a granular edge on top of the noise.
- **A suspended AudioContext accepts everything and plays nothing, without erroring.** From outside it is indistinguishable from a bug anywhere else in the chain, and it is why a replay counter can climb to 30 while the room stays silent. Report the context state, and resume defensively before playing.
- Instrument the settings that can silence the whole installation — master volume and context state — or a muted room looks exactly like a broken one.
- **Set level in one place.** A replayed whisper was pre-scaled to 0.12 in the sample buffer *and* passed through a gain of 0.1, arriving at about -44 dBFS — audible only in silence with an ear at the speaker, and nothing at all on a television. Nobody heard one for the entire life of the feature. Write buffers at full scale; the gain node does the level.
- **Sound memory rolls on its own clock.** Whisper replay used to sit at the bottom of `detectEmergentEvents`, below four early returns including the cap on concurrent visual recalls, so a room busy remembering things to look at was silent. The two kinds of memory are independent and must surface independently.
- The whisper band's upper bound rejects a shout; it must not reject ordinary speech. A flat 0.22 discarded exactly the loudest, most deliberate attempts to make the room hear something. `AudioDiagnostics.tooLoud` reports rejection-for-loudness so it can never again look like silence.
- **The microphone must be observable.** A silent room and a deaf one are indistinguishable from outside the machine, which is why this went unnoticed through several sessions. The instrument shows live level against threshold, whispers held, captured and replayed; the session log records all of it.
- `audition` plays everything held, spaced out, for commissioning. Not part of the artwork — a whisper should surface rarely and unbidden — but an operator has to be able to answer "is there anything in there at all", and waiting never answers that.

- The room tone and the whisper analyser occupy disjoint frequency bands (`TONE_BAND` below 150 Hz, `ANALYSIS_BAND` from 180 Hz). The speakers run continuously, so an overlap would either mask real whispers or manufacture them, and suppressing the microphone whenever the speakers were active would mean suppressing it permanently. The separation is physical, which is why the continuous tone needs no suppression window at all — unlike whisper replay, which still does.
- Restricting analysis to the voice band is also what keeps HVAC rumble and footfall out of the whisper threshold.
- Every scheduled gain change is ramped. A stepped gain is audible as a click in a quiet gallery.

## Detection accuracy

- **The number of people is bounded by how much of the room is moving.** Measured over a real session, one visitor ranged 39–546 active cells while every three-person claim sat at 75 cells or fewer: three bodies cannot move less than one. `maxPlausibleBodies` is a bound, not a detector — it only bites when the frame is nearly still, which is exactly when the shape-based tests have least to work with.
- Coalescing tracks needs three tests, not one: centroid distance, box overlap, and `isFragmentOf`. A hand or shoulder can register just outside a torso's box while obviously belonging to it, and neither distance nor overlap catches that. The fragment test is a heuristic about human proportions rather than a principle; it holds because a fragment is small and a person is not.
- **Two people cannot stand inside one body's width of each other.** Blob merging only sees what a single frame detected; it cannot help when a nearly-still visitor registers as disconnected scraps that never touch — a hand here, a shoulder there. On a real session 44 of 307 samples reported two or more people at a median coverage of 8%, which is one person barely moving. Tracks are coalesced after association, by centroid distance *and* by box overlap: a moving hand can sit well away from a body's centre while lying entirely inside its outline, and distance alone cannot see that.
- **A path that went nowhere was not walked twice.** `trajectoryDistance` between the two halves of a stationary visitor's path is near zero — their path is a cloud of points at one spot. Without `RETURN_MIN_EXTENT`, someone who simply sat down was told "you have walked this before" about eleven seconds after arriving. Any detector comparing a path to itself needs a reach requirement.
- **A track being born is not a person arriving.** Every track in the room is born in the same instant when sensing starts, and a track reappearing mid-room after an occlusion was never away. Treating the two as one event is a systematic false positive, not an occasional one: on a real session it told a single visitor "two of you, at once" within three seconds of starting. Arrival requires appearing near the edge of the frame.
- **The room makes no claims while sensing is settling.** After warmup, anything already standing in the room is learned into the background gradually, so the changed area starts at roughly twice its eventual size and falls for several seconds — measured at 42, 50, 45, 42, 38, 33, 28, 23 and then steady at 22. Detections in that window are worth reacting to visually and not worth announcing. `BehaviorWatcher` still records history across the boundary so detectors have continuity, but reports nothing, and `speak` refuses outright.
- **The size-scaled merge allowance is vertical only, and the asymmetry is the whole point.** The scale factor multiplies the *smaller* dimension of a region, and for a standing person that dimension is their width — so applying it sideways grew the allowance in exactly the direction that distinguishes two neighbours, and grew it fastest when they stood closest to the camera and were largest. Measured before the split: two visitors 0.28 wide had to stand 40% of the frame apart to be counted as two, against 14% for the same pair at gallery distance; afterwards both need only the fixed `BODY_MERGE_GAP`, which for the near pair is the width of their own bodies plus one and a half cells. Every side-by-side test in the suite had used bodies 0.07–0.13 wide, so desk range — the range a laptop camera actually works at — was the uncovered case. The horizontal allowance must stay `BODY_MERGE_GAP`; only `verticalGapFor` scales.
- **Where the two merge errors are not symmetric.** Over-counting one close visitor split down the middle is caught downstream by fragment continuity and track coalescing. Under-counting is silent: two people reported as one produces exactly what a quieter gallery produces. When a merge rule has to err, it errs toward two.
- **A person is a vertical column, and the merge rule must be asymmetric about that.** Their fragments stack — head above torso above legs — and share a horizontal band; two people stand side by side and share almost none. A symmetric gap cannot close the first without also closing the second. On a real session 51 samples reported two people when there was one, every one of them two genuinely separate regions at a modest 17.5% coverage. `COLUMN_MERGE_GAP` reaches much further vertically than anything reaches sideways.
- Connected components join only orthogonally touching cells, so one visitor routinely arrives as several fragments split wherever their clothing matches the background. `mergeAdjacentBlobs` closes gaps up to `BODY_MERGE_GAP` before tracking. Without it a single person was reported as three or four, and the artwork answered each fragment separately instead of answering the person.
- A detection must be seen `TRACK_CONFIRM_SIGHTINGS` times before it is a person. Minting a track from a single frame, and then granting it four seconds of occlusion prediction, turned one visitor into a lingering crowd of phantoms.
- Only confirmed tracks earn occlusion prediction. An unconfirmed candidate that vanishes was noise; holding it is what made the phantoms persist.
- `selectObservedTracks` is the single gate between sensing and the artwork: confirmed, currently observed. Nothing else may reach the swarm, the memory field, or the person count.
- Measured after these three changes on a live session: mean occluded tracks 0.00 and population churn 1.1%, against 3–4 lingering phantoms per visitor before.

## Stored settings

- **Bump `CONFIG_SCHEMA` whenever a setting changes meaning rather than value.** A saved config from an older schema is discarded whole, not merged. This is not defensive tidiness: `sceneChangeLimit` counted changed pixels and became a coverage threshold under the same key, so a stored `0.32` — entirely reasonable as a pixel fraction — was silently reapplied as a 32% coverage limit and rejected a third of every frame on a live installation. No amount of validating the number can catch that, because the number is still in range. Only versioning the meaning can.
- Prefer renaming a key outright when its semantics change. A rename makes stale data unreadable; a version bump makes it discardable. Doing both is cheap.
- **A control's label must name what rises when the slider rises.** `ghostFrequency` and `whisperFrequency` were both labelled "rarity" while holding a frequency, so the operator who wanted whispers *less rare* dragged toward zero and switched them off. `every slider writes to its own configuration key` could not catch it — the binding was correct — so `a slider labelled for more of something sends more of it` pins the direction as well as the key.
- **Why the room declined is a value, not an absence.** `chooseWhisperRecall` returns a named refusal — `nothing held`, `frequency is zero`, `not this time`, `nothing ripe yet` — and the D panel prints it beside the whisper counters. The decision used to be four early returns inside the render component, which made the operator's actual question ("they are in there, the console plays them, why does the room never say one?") answerable only with a debugger and a gallery. Same reasoning that put `assessLiveness` in `liveness.ts`.
- **Zero on a frequency is off, not "as rare as possible", and the room must say so.** Silence is what this installation produces when it is working, so an off switch is indistinguishable from art unless the console admits it. The audition button keeps playing whispers at zero, which makes the failure read as working audio and a silent piece — the most misleading combination available. Any future setting whose zero disables a behaviour needs the same notice.

## Defaults

- The hardware profile is **detected, not hardcoded**. A fixed CHEAP default is the safe guess for an unknown computer and the wrong one for nearly every machine this runs on: on a real session it capped sensing at 9.3 Hz, slow enough that the two-frame persistence rule demanded over 200 ms of held movement. `chooseHardwareProfile` moves up from CHEAP only on evidence, and never overrides a profile an operator has saved.
- FULL carries 360,000 particles. Measured on Apple silicon through ANGLE's Metal backend: 60 fps at both 1080p and 3840×2017. Raised from 262k on that measurement. The cost is graininess at 4K, where the same count covers four times the pixels.
- The memory half-life is 120 minutes, not 240, and the reason is structure rather than speed. Measured over a 12-hour virtual day, a 240-minute half-life lets the field saturate to 0.74 mean density and its mean gradient falls 30% below its three-hour peak; at 120 minutes the field holds 0.16 against a 0.19 peak. The swarm follows the gradient of that field, so a saturated field is a flat flow and the artwork stops expressing memory.
- **Deposit rates were examined and deliberately left alone.** Reducing them by a third or a sixth was measured and made things worse: mean field gradient at 30 minutes dropped from 0.163 to 0.069 and 0.035. The saturation concern that prompted the experiment was wrong.
- `burn-in.ts` takes its decay from `DEFAULT_CONFIG` rather than a literal, so the release gate exercises the configuration that actually ships.

## Language and runtime choices

- **Rust was evaluated for the sensing pipeline and rejected on measurement.** The whole CPU path — background model, thresholding, cell accumulation, connected components, plus luminance conversion — costs about 108 µs per frame at 160×90. At 20 Hz that is 0.17% of one core, with roughly 6x headroom before it reaches 1%. A WASM port would save a fraction of a percent of a core and cost a toolchain, a build step, and a marshalling boundary. Do not port it for speed.
- The accuracy ceiling is algorithmic, not computational. A higher analysis resolution, a per-pixel Gaussian mixture background, or Kalman-plus-assignment tracking are all affordable in JavaScript at this scale; reach for those before reaching for a different language.
- The case where Rust does pay off is **replacing the browser**, not accelerating code inside it: a native runtime for a multi-projector, floor-and-mirror installation, where the browser's autoplay gesture, tab throttling, single-surface output and compositor latency all become real constraints. That is a runtime decision for the scaled-up install, not an optimisation of this one.
- The burn-in gate runs in 0.45 s. There is no dev-loop pressure to move tooling either.

## Diagnosing sensing

- Sensing failure is silent by construction: a camera that sees nothing renders exactly what an empty gallery renders. Every stage of the pipeline must therefore be observable from inside the running installation, on its own key (**D**), without a console or a debugger.
- `diagnoseSensing` reports the **first** stalled stage, never the last. Downstream stages are starved by definition, so "no tracks" is true and useless when the camera never opened.
- Every finding must carry an actionable fix with the actual numbers in it, not a status. `sensing-diagnosis.test.ts` fails a finding whose advice is too short to act on.
- The instrument shows sensor space and projection space side by side. Undetected and detected-in-the-wrong-place produce the same symptom on the wall and have completely different causes.
- Measure the sensing loop separately from the render loop. They fail independently, and a stalled sensing loop at 60 fps looks perfectly healthy from the front.
- Rate-limit an analysis loop in one place. A `setInterval(42)` gated by a `>= 42 ms` test loses a tick to timer jitter about half the time: FULL advertised 24 Hz and delivered 16. Tick fast and let the elapsed check be the only limiter.
- `sceneChangeLimit` is configurable because the correct value depends on how much of the frame a person occupies. The 0.32 default assumes a camera looking across a room; at desk range it rejects every frame.
- Sensing telemetry (`SensingSample`) records aggregate counts only — no coordinates, no imagery — which is why it can run continuously rather than behind the opt-in the anonymous event recorder needs. Export it from the instrument and read it with `npm run analyze-session`. A snapshot cannot show churn, intermittent stalls, or a guard that only trips when someone walks close.
- The instrument answers "is the room seeing me" at the laptop. **T** answers it while standing in front of the wall, which is where the question is actually asked. Keep that overlay visually unlike the artwork — hard edges, high contrast — so it can never be mistaken for the piece.
- A chosen camera is requested with `deviceId: { exact }`. Falling back to the built-in camera after an operator picked an external one yields a plausible picture from the wrong lens, which is harder to notice than a failure.

## Sensor stability

- **The frame-wide light-shift estimate must be robust, not the arithmetic mean.** The mean is destroyed by any large nearby object: a visitor covering a third of the frame and 120 levels brighter than the wall drags it to ~41, so every *untouched* background pixel then differs from its corrected background by more than the threshold and the entire image reads as changed. Measured on a real desk-range session, this produced ~29% of pixels changing continuously, a third of the grid lit at all times, and 21% of frames discarded. `medianDifference` uses a histogram median, which a minority of the frame moving cannot move, at a cost of about 10 µs per frame.
- **The scene-change guard tests spatial coverage, not changed pixels.** The two are not the same, and conflating them was the defect above compounded: one person close to the camera changes a large share of the pixels while occupying only part of the room. A genuine lighting change lights up essentially every cell. In the session that exposed this, a single visitor lit 27–56% of the grid and never approached the 85% coverage threshold, where the old 32% pixel-fraction guard rejected a fifth of all frames.
- The merge allowance for body fragments scales with the size of the regions being merged. A fixed gap only suits bodies at one distance; a visitor close to the camera arrives as large fragments separated by correspondingly large gaps.
- The motion background model compensates for frame-wide light shifts, rejects excessive grid coverage (85% by default) or more than 92% residual changed pixels, and requires a cell to remain active for two analyzed frames. These three mechanisms prevent auto-exposure, projector transitions, and single-frame camera noise from creating paths.
- Changed pixels learn into the background at a much slower rate than quiet pixels. This prevents a stationary visitor from being learned as the room and producing a negative ghost when they leave.
- Live diagnostics may display only the ephemeral 40×23 motion-cell mask and aggregate counts. Never place camera pixels in the operator console, logs, exports, or persisted state.
- The same 40×23 mask now also drives a visitor's silhouette on the projection, which is a deliberate widening of where it may appear. It remains an occupancy grid, never camera pixels, is far too coarse to identify anyone, and is never written. Seeing your own outline is what makes the room's response attributable to you; showing your image is not required to achieve it, and is still forbidden.
- `saveRoom` writes through the `PERSISTED_ROOM_KEYS` allowlist. Because sensor-derived imagery now travels further through the app, a future refactor handing `saveRoom` a richer object must not be able to persist it by accident; `persistence.test.ts` proves a polluted snapshot is stripped.

## Runtime boundary

- **The exhibition runtime is a static export served on `127.0.0.1` by `scripts/static-server.mjs`, and only that.** Keep it dependency-free and offline. A published copy exists as well (below); it is a second runtime for a different purpose, never the one a gallery runs on.
- Do not replace the static server with Wrangler for exhibition use. Wrangler's development runtime exhausted the process file-watcher limit and attempted writes outside the project on this machine; the dependency-free server avoids both failure modes.
- `npm run exhibit -- --kiosk` is the unattended entry point. It builds the static artifact, starts the local server, and restarts the server after failure.
- **A device that drops is released where it drops, not at unmount.** `microphoneStreamRef` holds one stream and the 2.5 s retry overwrites it, so stopping tracks only in the effect cleanup released the newest microphone and abandoned every earlier one — a leak proportional to how often a USB device disconnects, which over twelve hours is not rare. The single-drop test could not see this: unmount stopped the one stream the ref still held. Only a second drop separates "the current stream is released" from "every stream is", so the regression test drops the microphone three times.

## The published copy

- `npm run deploy` publishes the built room to Cloudflare Pages at `the-room-remembers-you.pages.dev`. It is for opening the piece from somewhere that is not the gallery. It does not change what the gallery machine runs, and the offline exhibition path above still has no network dependency.
- **The build directory is not the publish directory.** Vite and vinext leave `.vite/manifest.json`, `.assetsignore` and `vinext-client-entry-manifest.json` beside the artifact. Workers hosting skips them because `.assetsignore` names them; **Pages has no equivalent mechanism and uploads whatever directory it is handed**, so a plain `wrangler pages deploy dist/client` publishes the exact chunk-and-entry-point map of the build. `scripts/deploy-pages.mjs` stages a copy instead, and `lib/pages-publish.ts` decides what is withheld so the decision is testable rather than a shell incantation.
- The staging copy is also why `_headers` is edited outside `dist/`. In place, the next build reverts it and the publish after that appends to an already-rewritten file; `withExhibitionHeaders` is idempotent for the same reason.
- The hosted copy must send `Permissions-Policy: camera=(self), microphone=(self)` to match the local server, or it is a page that cannot see or hear. Pages serves HTTPS, so the secure-context requirement for `getUserMedia` is satisfied there in a way a plain LAN address would not be.
- **On the hosted copy the heartbeat leaves the machine.** `room-installation.tsx` posts to `/heartbeat` every few seconds; the launcher consumes it locally, and Pages answers 405 — but the body still crosses the network to Cloudflare's edge before being rejected. It carries aggregate numbers only (fps, sensing rate, track count, `entered`, engine), no coordinates and no imagery, so the privacy invariants hold. It is nonetheless the one respect in which the published copy is not offline. Gate the fetch on the hostname if that ever needs to be strictly true.

## What belongs outside the render component

- **A decision that lives inside `room-installation.tsx` is a decision no test can reach.** Every defect this session chased had that shape: the whisper recall gate was four early returns in a callback, the merge allowance was reachable only by standing in front of a camera, and the debug-event validator could be asked what it rejects only by constructing a `File` and running a browser. The component is where React state, refs and device handles live; arithmetic, validation and choice belong in `lib/` where they can be called directly. Same reasoning that put `assessLiveness` in `liveness.ts` rather than in the launcher.
- The seams that came out, and what each one made checkable: `room-projection.ts` (rotate-then-calibrate, non-commutative and previously verifiable only by eye), `memory-records.ts` (defaults for rooms saved by older builds, where a missing `strength` becomes `NaN` and renders as nothing), `whisper-recall.ts` (named refusals), `session-export.ts` (the only untrusted input the installation has), `calibration-flow.ts` (marker numbering and the partial quad that still solves), `room-canvas.ts` and `room-drawing.ts` (whether anything was drawn at all). The rest came out afterwards, and the rule is now enforced rather than remembered — see *Modularity is the standard* below.
- **A drawing function that quietly draws nothing is indistinguishable from an empty gallery.** `test-doubles/canvas-2d.ts` records calls instead of rasterising, which answers "did this paint, and how much" — never "did it look right", which is what the reference captures in `docs/` are for. Both checks are needed: the standards already record two defects that passed every numerical test and were visible only in a screenshot, and this catches the opposite failure.
- Keep the DOM boundary thin and on its own. `browser-download.ts` is twenty lines because everything about *what* a file contains is in `session-export.ts`, and only the part that genuinely needs a browser needs a browser to test.

## Surviving a gallery day

- **The rule about silent sensing failure applies to the renderer, and did not.** "A camera that sees nothing renders exactly what an empty gallery renders" is one stage too early: this room is dark by design and lit only by the field, so a renderer that has stopped looks precisely like ground nobody has walked on. A lost WebGL context, a render error, and a frozen draw loop each took the whole installation with nothing on the wall, in the console or in the logs to say so.
- Cancelling `webglcontextlost` only *requests* a restore. A driver that has given up sends no `webglcontextrestored` at all, so the never-arrives case needs a timeout; no listener can report it.
- `RendererHealth` abandons the swarm after repeated losses rather than recovering forever. Each rebuild reseeds the particles, so recovering several times a minute is strobing, and strobing reads as a fault rather than as the work. `SwarmRenderer` holds every GL resource in readonly fields built by its constructor, which is what makes rebuilding the object the honest recovery and patching a half-dead one the dishonest one.
- **A "not lost" sentinel of 0 collides with a real timestamp of 0.** The first version of `RendererHealth` used `lostAt = 0` to mean healthy, so a context lost at that instant reported itself as holding one and could never time out. A regression test caught it. Use null.
- **The heartbeat must measure frames itself, never read `status.fps`.** That average is written *by* the draw loop, so a stopped loop leaves its last healthy value sitting there to be reported forever — the one failure the heartbeat exists to catch is the one it would have missed. Count frames, divide by the watcher's own elapsed time.
- The heartbeat effect must not depend on `entered`. Restarting it resets the frame window, so the first beat after a visitor stepped in measured no elapsed time, reported zero frames a second, and would have had the launcher relaunch Chrome in front of them.
- The launcher watched the static file server — no dependencies, no state, the least likely component here to fail — and never watched the page holding the camera, the microphone and twelve hours of memory. `open -a "Google Chrome"` returns no process handle, which is why the browser could be started once and never afterwards managed. Spawn Chrome directly, against a *stable* profile directory: a fresh profile per launch would put a camera permission prompt on the gallery wall.
- Four missed beats before presuming the page is gone, not one. A long GC pause or a starved timer loses a beat, and taking the room away from a visitor standing in front of it costs more than waiting one more cycle.
- Frame rate is only judged once a visitor has passed the threshold screen; before that the room is a static page and is supposed to be.

## Placing sound in the room

- The installation placed two kinds of sound and they disagreed. A remembered whisper went through a `StereoPannerNode`; the room's own sentence went through a bare `HTMLAudioElement`, which takes a volume and knows nothing about position — so the line was drawn beside a visitor and spoken from the middle of the speakers. `stereoPanFor` is now shared by both, and a test asserts a memory and a sentence about the same spot produce the same pan.
- The rendered voice plays through the `AudioMemoryEngine`'s own context: one sample rate, one master path, one mix. The context is fetched at play time because it does not exist until the microphone has started, while `RoomSpeech` is built before that and rebuilt on every microphone retry.
- The pan limit stays at 0.75. A fully panned source is silent in the other channel, and a memory inaudible to the person it belongs to has failed at its only purpose.
- **This is a claim about speakers as much as about code.** On a television's built-in pair, 60 cm apart and three metres away, every panned sound still arrives from one point. The mapping is only audible with two powered speakers separated across the space. Do not describe the piece as spatial audio without them.
- `.wav` was missing from the static server's media types, so 34 files the room's voice depends on were served as `application/octet-stream` for the browser to sniff.

## The room's interest

- Interest is earned from behaviour and dies with the track. It is never stored, never carried to a later visit, and never handed to whoever is nearest — so a visitor who leaves and returns is a new presence, because that is all the sensing can honestly support.
- The refractory period is what makes this preference rather than tracking. Without it the room attends to whoever meets the threshold next, continuously, which is what a security camera does and reads as one.
- Evidence is an event, not a state. Rate-limit it at the call site: a standing visitor would otherwise file one reason to be interesting on every sensing tick — 24 a second on FULL — and cross any threshold instantly. Evidence also expires, or everyone in a gallery eventually qualifies by accumulation alone.
- The recall bias chooses *which* memory surfaces and never how often one does, so every measured bound on the room's pace — recall rate, crossing cooldown, the 10% disturbed-occupancy share — stands exactly where it was measured. Prefer biasing selection over biasing rate for this reason.
- `RoomVoice`'s minimum gap is untouched by interest; only the chance of taking an already permitted occasion rises. Interest makes the room's one line more likely to be *about* someone, never makes it speak more often.
- **The burn-in's first attention gate was not a gate.** Measured against the churning gallery simulation it read 0.005 and moved only to 0.009 when the refractory period was deleted outright, because what limits attention in that traffic is that the simulated visitors do not stay: tracks end before evidence accumulates and interest dies with them. A bound the constant it polices cannot move is a number that happens to be small. The tuning is now measured against the case it exists for — one visitor who never leaves — which goes from 0.117 to 0.968 when the rest period is removed.
- That measurement then failed its own constant. A spell is attack + hold + fade = 64 s, so the ceiling is 64 / (64 + rest); at six minutes it is 0.151, *above* the 0.15 share the gate allows, and it passed only on the rounding of accumulated seconds. The rest period was set from the bound rather than the bound from the rest period.

## Removing a dead setting

- `audioRecursion` was written by two call sites, clamped by a setter, and read by nothing. The operator console's slider for it had already been removed, leaving an orphaned note describing behaviour that did not exist, and the README documented it as a setup step. A control that does nothing is worse than an undocumented one.
- **Removing a never-read key does not need a `CONFIG_SCHEMA` bump.** The rule exists to stop a stored value being reinterpreted under a changed meaning; a key nothing reads cannot be misinterpreted, and it is inert in a saved config. Bumping would have discarded every operator's saved settings — including the four-point spatial calibration — to tidy away something harmless. Bump for changed meaning, not for tidiness.

## Body scale, and constants that encode a camera position

- **`CELLS_PER_PERSON = 55` was a measurement of one camera distance, applied as if it were a property of people.** It came from desk-range sessions, where a single visitor filled 39–546 cells of the 40×23 grid. At the geometry the README actually prescribes — mounted high, looking across a gallery — a body covers about 7. The bound `activeCells / 55` therefore divided by a number eight times too large everywhere the work was meant to be installed.
- Measured on synthetic gallery frames, against the committed code: **two visitors 61 px apart were reported as one person.** Six were one. Eight were 1.8. The failure is silent in every channel the installation has — no rejected frames, no fault, nothing different on the wall — because under-counting people produces exactly what a quiet gallery produces.
- The consequence was not only the count. Every two-person behaviour the work has — approach, together, mirror, follow — needs two confirmed tracks, so roughly half the room's repertoire was unreachable at its own prescribed geometry.
- `BodyScale` measures the figure from the scene: a median of merged-region cell counts over a 30 s window. A median for the same reason `medianDifference` is one — a minority of samples must not move it. A body that briefly fragments contributes several small samples; two visitors who touch contribute one large one; neither drags an estimate a hundred ordinary observations are holding.
- **Until the scale has evidence, the bound is not applied at all.** The asymmetry is the point: a bound with the wrong scale silently deletes people who are standing in the room, while no bound merely permits an over-count — and over-counting is what fragment continuity, column merging and box-overlap coalescing were all built to catch. Fail toward the mechanisms that have evidence behind them.
- The scale is learned *after* the bound is applied to a frame, never before, so a frame the bound truncated cannot also teach the bound that bodies are larger than they are.
- `reset()` (Relearn empty room) discards the scale along with the background. An operator relearns the room after moving the camera, and moving the camera is precisely what changes body size in the frame.
- The per-frame ceiling was 8, which made the FULL profile's advertised 12 unreachable — the documentation described a limit the code could not deliver. `MAX_DETECTED_BODIES` is now 12, matching the profile and the shader's twelve body slots.
- **Two crowd limits are physical and must not be engineered away.** Visitors shoulder to shoulder are one connected silhouette, and so are two people the camera sees one directly behind the other — the column-merge rule that correctly joins one person's head, torso and legs cannot distinguish that from two stacked bodies. A single view does not contain the information. Do not add a heuristic that guesses.
- The scene-change guard was the suspected crowd failure and measurement cleared it: a room of twenty-five reaches about 27% grid coverage against an 85% limit. `crowd-sensing.test.ts` records that margin so a future change to the guard has to face it. **The hypothesis was wrong and the measurement found a worse bug than the one being looked for** — which is the argument for building the harness before theorising.
- `crowd-sensing.test.ts` is synthetic and cannot speak to lighting, lenses, clothing, or a real gallery background. It exists to hold the *geometry* fixed. Validating with actual hardware in the actual room is still required, and is still the thing most likely to surprise.

## The DOM fixture

- **Import the fixture before any component.** React DOM chooses its event implementation at import time, and Base UI resolves the document its portals attach to the same way. A test file that imports a component first renders it into nothing at all — no portal, no error, no warning, an empty container and a passing render call. This cost an hour to find and is invisible in a stack trace, which is why `dom.ts` imports React DOM dynamically and why every suite lists the fixture first.
- **jsdom's answer for `:fullscreen`, `:modal` and `:popover-open` is `false`, and it walks the document to get there.** Opening one popover measured 110,613,467 `:fullscreen` matches and twenty seconds, entirely inside nwsapi. Answering the three directly leaves every result unchanged and took the overlay suite from 44 s to 80 ms. This is a statement about the selector engine, not a claim that the fixture models the top layer; nothing may test fullscreen behaviour through it.
- `getAnimations` has to exist — a scroll area calls it from a timer, where a TypeError kills the process instead of failing a test — and once it exists, Base UI takes its wait-for-the-exit-animation path and a closed popup never settles. Its own `BASE_UI_ANIMATIONS_DISABLED` flag is set for that reason.
- Observers are read from the element's own window, not from `globalThis`, so `ResizeObserver` and `IntersectionObserver` must exist in both places or a panel group throws on mount. `DOMRect`, `NodeFilter`, `DataTransfer` and the event constructors are likewise reached as bare globals.
- A stand-in for a platform object must be a **Proxy, not an `Object.create`**: jsdom's `Navigator` and Node's `Performance` expose everything through getters that must run against the real instance, and an `Object.create` with one as its prototype either throws `ERR_INVALID_THIS` or silently yields `undefined`. A `navigator` built that way stopped Base UI rendering entirely, with no error.
- Replacing `performance` wholesale breaks React's development build, which reaches for `performance.mark`. Replace `now` and forward the rest.
- Writing to `input.value` does nothing useful: React installs its own setter, so the field shows the text and every handler is told nothing. Write through the prototype's setter — that is what `type()` in the fixture is for. Listbox options commit on `pointerup` and swallow the click that follows, which is what `pointerClick()` is for.
- Stylesheet imports are resolved to an empty module by `style-imports.ts`. Without it `app/layout.tsx` could not be imported by any test at all, because Node refuses the extension outright.

## Running the whole installation in a test

- `test-doubles/installation.ts` mounts the real component with a fake camera, microphone, GPU, store and clock. The camera's scene is a luminance function, so a test moves a visitor by replacing it. Nothing here can reach a real device.
- **The room has two loops and they fail independently**: the draw loop runs on a frame, sensing on a 12 ms interval. The harness owns both, and `advance()` steps them together because a browser does. `tick()` alone runs the intervals — which is also how the once-a-second status publish is made deterministic instead of a test sleeping through it.
- The harness holds `requestAnimationFrame` on the window as well as on `globalThis`, because a portal opening and a focus trap wait behind the same clock the draw loop uses. `paint()` exists so an interaction is followed by a frame, as it would be in a browser.
- **Figures are sized to the geometry the work prescribes**, about seven cells of the 40x23 grid. A desk-range figure filling a third of the frame is a different scene, and measuring against one is exactly how `CELLS_PER_PERSON` came to encode one camera distance as a property of people.
- The 2D recorder answers `createRadialGradient` and `measureText`. Returning nothing for them threw inside the trace renderer's own draw loop, which is how a fallback surface stays untested.

## Counting people

- **`maxPlausibleBodies` rounds; it must not floor.** A body's accepted cells are fewer than the merged region `BodyScale` learned from, so two visitors land a little under twice the scale far more often than over it, and flooring always resolves that shortfall by deleting somebody. Measured by driving the whole installation: two gallery-scale visitors produced 19 accepted cells against a learned scale of 11 — 1.73 bodies — and the room reported one person. That is the silent under-count this repository already describes, arriving through the mechanism built to prevent an over-count.
- Rounding keeps every case the bound exists for. The real session that claimed three people at 75 cells against a 55-cell scale is 1.36 and still rounds to one; a nearly-still frame still floors at one through the `Math.max`. The asymmetry is deliberate and is the same one the scale itself uses: an over-count is what fragment continuity, column merging and box-overlap coalescing exist to catch, and an under-count has nothing behind it.
- `memory-engine.test.ts` sweeps every cell count from 0 to 400 at three scales and requires the bound never to fall below the floored value, so no future change can reintroduce a downward bias without failing.

## Mutation testing

- `npm run test:mutation` runs `scripts/mutation.mjs`, which mutates lib, the five installation components and the scripts that decide something, **in groups**. One configuration covering everything produced 7,342 mutants against a command runner that cannot know which file a mutant is in, so each one ran all 549 tests — nineteen seconds apiece, about six hours. A gate nobody runs is a suggestion.
- Each group pairs sources with the suites that exercise them. The mapping is deliberately generous: a group that is too narrow reports a survivor the full suite would have killed, which is a false alarm, while one that is too wide only costs time. `--group=<name>` runs one, `--list` prints them, and each keeps an incremental file so a re-run only re-tests what changed.
- **`swarm-renderer.ts` is excluded, and the reason is not that it is hard.** A Node test runner cannot observe a mutant inside a shader, so scoring it reported 12.5% with 314 survivors — a number for code that was never run, which is as dishonest as scoring it 100%. What establishes the renderer is `npm run test:render`. The same reasoning excludes `scripts/render-regression/browser.ts`.
- The first wide run found two real gaps in the palette. Nothing asserted a mark's **composite mode**, which is the polarity of the whole work: a `source-over` mark in a dark room paints the room's own black over what is on the wall, and a `lighter` mark on paper brightens a near-white ground and vanishes. Both render, and the contrast floors cannot tell them apart because they read the declared colour rather than what reaches the wall. And the **colour space conversion was only ever compared to itself**, so a wrong matrix coefficient moved every measurement together and every floor kept passing; it is now anchored to published sRGB and CIE values.

## Deleting a guard rather than testing around it

- A branch no state the code can reach will satisfy is not a check, it is an untestable claim, and the coverage gate cannot pass with one in place. Four were removed rather than faked: `SessionAudio`'s is-it-empty break (iterating the map terminates by construction), `rememberWhisper`'s neighbour fallback (index 0 reads itself and every other index is in range), `medianDifference`'s trailing return (every sample lands in a bin, so the running total always passes half the frame), and `MotionFrameAnalyzer`'s empty-rejection-window branch (the frame's own verdict is pushed before the diagnostics are built).
- Where the guard protects against data nobody has written yet, give it a seam instead of deleting it: `sampleSwarmGradient` takes its stops, and `RoomVoice` takes its vocabulary. Both cases are about a future table — a duplicated gradient stop, a kind down to its last line — and both would otherwise be claims no test could make.
- The launcher exited twenty seconds after SIGTERM, waiting on a pooled probe socket and a child handle rather than on anything it still needed. launchd will not start the next instance until this one is gone, so that was twenty seconds of black wall on every restart. Probes now use an agent with no keep-alive and `stop()` exits rather than draining the loop.

## Installation module boundaries

- Projection (`room-projection`), Canvas drawing (`room-canvas`, `room-drawing`), body composition (`room-bodies`), stored-record normalization (`memory-records`), ended-track capture (`room-capture`), and aggregate telemetry (`room-telemetry`) are independently callable modules. Pass the appropriate clock value into capture and composition: persisted capture uses wall-clock milliseconds, while active recall presentation uses the animation clock. Do not make a pure module read React refs to recover those inputs.
- `createRuntimeStatus` returns fresh nested state, including its motion mask. Shared initial diagnostic objects would let a reset or a second installation inherit mutations from the first; `room-modules.test.ts` checks isolation. Telemetry uses an explicit field allowlist and its test rejects added sensor fields.
- Demo pointer expiry belongs to the analysis clock (`PointerPresence`), not independent browser timeouts. Coverage exposed the old fixture advancing 1.2 virtual seconds while its pointer survived on a real 180 ms timer; it passed when fast and failed under instrumentation. The integration test now sends continuous movement, and unit tests pin expiry at exactly 180 ms, renewal, and copied input ownership. Per-event timeouts also let an older event erase a newer one, which the clock-based module avoids.

## Modularity is the standard, and `module-boundaries.test.ts` is the check

- **A decision reachable only by mounting the component is a decision that is not checked.** This was already written down and it held only as long as somebody remembered it, so it is now enforced. `module-boundaries.test.ts` asserts four things: every module in `lib/` is imported *directly* by some test, nothing in `lib/` imports React or a component, `room-installation.tsx` stays under a line ceiling, and the specific decisions that were taken out of it have not come back. The ceiling ratchets — lower it when work removes something, never raise it to make room. Anything that would need it raised is a decision that belongs in a module.
- **Transitive coverage is a different claim from a test.** A module exercised only through its callers is tested at whatever inputs those callers happen to produce, which is precisely the position the render component put every one of these in. Hence "imported directly", not "covered".
- What came out this round, and what each one made checkable: `room-emergence` (the six-branch recall decision and the order it applies them in), `room-upkeep` (the fifteen-second forgetting loop and the saved snapshot), `room-frame` (the layer-by-layer composite, against the recording canvas), `room-lifecycle` (opening, closing, and the night), `room-keys` (what every printed shortcut does), `sensor-inputs` (which bodies the loop is looking at and what it calls them), `debug-recorder` (the anonymous recording and its replay cursor), `attention-evidence` (how rarely dwelling and stopping are filed), `rate-meter` (both loops' reported rates and the render load), `media-devices` (what the browser is asked for and what the operator is told when it refuses), `projection-surface` (pixel budget and keystone), and `SensingLog` (the telemetry cadence and its bound).
- Effects come back as values. `RoomEmergence.detect` mutates the arrays it is handed — those are the objects the renderer is drawing — and *returns* the occasions to speak and the one status line. The component applies them. That is what makes "a crossing must not pre-empt stillness" a unit test instead of a gallery visit.
- **A new module is unmutated until it is put in a group.** `scripts/mutation.mjs` mutates the sources it is told to, so a module added to `lib/` without an entry in `GROUPS` passes the mutation gate by never being tested by it. The two new groups are `room-logic` (the decisions) and `room-surface` (the presentation and the numbers the room reports about itself); adding a module means adding it there in the same change. Also: the runner keeps a per-group incremental file, so a run after new tests land can report the *old* survivors in seconds — delete `reports/mutation/incremental-<group>.json` before trusting a score that did not move.
- Scores as measured: `room-logic` 96.1% (`room-emergence` 92.9%, the rest 100%), `room-surface` 98.5%. Both clear the 90% break threshold. `room-modules` sits at 43.8% and `installation` is unmeasured; both were in that state beforehand — `room-modules` measured 41.1% at the commit before this work — and neither is a claim this change makes.
- Injected randomness, injected clocks, no imported devices. `EmergenceRoom` takes `random`, `now` and `wall`; `WhisperAudio` is the two methods the decision actually uses, so a test hands it a recorder rather than an `AudioContext`.

## Defects the extraction exposed

Each of these was invisible while the code lived in the render component, and each is now pinned by a test in the module that owns it.

- **A clock mixed across a module boundary.** Restoring a saved session seeded the anonymous recording's origin from `Date.now()` and then stamped new events with `performance.now()`. Every event recorded after a restore carried a `t` of roughly minus fifty years, the exporter wrote it out as fact, and the replay cursor read the whole file as already past and fired it on the first tick. `DebugRecorder` owns one clock and the caller passes it the monotonic one.
- **Zero is a clock reading, not the absence of one.** Five `useRef(0)` gates compared an unset zero against the page clock, which made every one of them a warmup as well as a rate: no attention evidence could be filed in the first twenty seconds of a session, no stillness recall in the first ninety, and the memory field could not be painted in the first seven-tenths of a second — on a `requestAnimationFrame` clock that starts at zero. They hold `null` now. Treat this as a class, not five instances: a "last time X happened" field initialised to a valid clock value is a bug wherever it appears.
- **Two bounds on the same ring.** Recording capped the anonymous log at 3,600 events; restoring one took the last 36,000. A room reopened after a long day came back holding ten times the recording it is allowed to keep, against the documented 2 MiB bound on serialized state. Both paths now use `DEBUG_EVENT_LIMIT`.
- **A rate reported as the mean of reciprocals.** The render loop averaged `1000 / elapsed` per frame, which the September 14 log follow-up had already identified as the wrong arithmetic for the sensing loop and which nothing had fixed for the display. Fifty-nine good frames and one 200 ms stall reads as 61 Hz that way and 52 Hz counted properly — the stall disappears from the instrument that exists to show it. `RateMeter` counts samples over elapsed time for both loops.
- **A memory with no path.** `normalizeGhost` supplies a missing `strength` but cannot invent a path, and a pathless ghost restored from an older build reaches `createFalseMemory`, which reads its first point and throws. Three `?? { x: 0.5, y: 0.5 }` fallbacks downstream were somebody noticing the shape of this without finding it. `isRememberable` drops the record at the door instead, and the fallbacks are gone.
- **Two drawing functions that could mutate their input.** `drawTracks` and `drawTrackingOverlay` took mutable `VisitorTrack[]`. Nothing was rewriting a track, but nothing said it could not; they take `readonly` arrays now, which is what let the frame composite be handed the live array without a copy.
- **An unconfirmed destructive control.** The console's "Forget everything" had no test at any level — the reset tests all went through the keyboard chord. A confirmation dialog that opens and whose action does nothing leaves an operator believing the room was cleared. It is covered now, including declining it.

## The Rust half, and what a parity report is allowed to claim

- **A port is not a migration.** `room_engine::tracking` reproduces `AnonymousTrackManager` and agrees with it on 2,480 frames across eight synthetic sessions, and the installation still calls the TypeScript. Agreeing on synthetic sessions and surviving a gallery are different claims, and only the second one is allowed to retire code. `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md` records which subsystems have moved, which have not, and what "moved" means for each.
- **Measure before claiming a language bought anything.** Tracking costs 0.0040 ms a frame in TypeScript and 0.0033 ms in WASM including every copy across the boundary; the field step is 0.0033 against 0.0031. At 24 Hz both are under a tenth of one percent of a frame. Rust is not faster here in any sense the artwork can feel, and the reason to have it is a deterministic core that can be replayed and debugged without a browser. The same measurement is why the sensing pipeline stayed in TypeScript, and that decision is unchanged.
- **Cross-implementation parity needs three legs, not two.** TypeScript against WASM answers "did the migration preserve the artwork". Native against WASM answers "does a session replayed on a developer's machine match the one the gallery ran" — they reach different `libm` implementations, and "it is the same source" is not evidence. Both run in `npm run test:parity`.
- **`serde_json` does not always parse an `f64` to the nearest representable value.** Measured on 1.0.151: `0.20400000000000001` — which is what `JSON.stringify` writes for an ordinary room coordinate — comes back as `0.204`, one unit in the last place low, where `str::parse::<f64>` is exact. The tracking parity gate reported sixty-nine disagreements that were entirely its own transport handing the native leg a different session; it now writes scenarios as raw little-endian `f64`. Writing is safe in the other direction, because `ryu`'s shortest form round trips through V8's correctly-rounded parser. Validate the instrument before theorising about the measurement.
- **The same rounding applies to every saved room**, because persistence is JSON. Coordinates restore to within one ULP, not exactly, and `a_saved_coordinate_survives_json_to_within_one_unit_in_the_last_place` pins that rather than the tempting `assert_eq!`, which passes for hand-picked values and fails on a real room. Artistically it is nothing — one ULP is 3e-17 of a wall. For deterministic replay it is one more reason a reproduction needs the anonymous observation log rather than a restored room.
- **A boundary with no type system spanning it needs a test that reads both sides.** The track record layout is declared once in `track_engine.rs` and mirrored in `track-bridge.ts`; `track-bridge.test.ts` parses the Rust source and compares the two field tables. A field added on one side then fails a test instead of silently decoding a confidence as a dwell time.
- **The gates were suggestions until they passed.** `cargo fmt --check` and `cargo clippy --workspace --all-targets -- -D warnings` are named in the V2 spec and neither had ever run clean: fourteen unformatted files and seven denials, three of which only appeared once formatting had split the lines hiding them. They are `npm run test:rust` now, inside `npm run check`. Frontend iteration is untouched — `npm run dev`, `npm test` and `npm run typecheck` still need no Rust toolchain, which is why the parity harnesses live outside the main `tsconfig.json`.
- **The tracker refuses a clock that goes backwards; the TypeScript accepts it.** This is the one deliberate behavioural divergence so far. A negative interval is floored to a single frame, which turns a clock jump into an enormous velocity and an association gate wide enough to swallow the room. The shell passes a monotonic clock, so it can only mean a bug, and `FrameError::Backwards` is a better outcome than a room that briefly believes everyone teleported.

## Two renderers, one artwork

- **WebGL2 is the exhibition default and `auto` never selects WebGPU.** Not because WebGPU fails — it is at visual parity, measured below — but because "it renders correctly" is one of five gates. Device-loss recovery, GPU memory over a twelve-hour day, frame pacing, and a soak on the machine the gallery actually uses are the other four, and none of them is done. `chooseRendererBackend` encodes that as a rule rather than a preference, and `room-renderer.test.ts` fails if `auto` ever starts choosing WebGPU on capability alone.
- **Visual parity is statistical and stated as such.** The two backends run different population sizes and scatter respawns from different hashes, so they are two draws from one distribution rather than two copies of one image. What must match is what a visitor could see: across CHEAP and FULL, room and paper, the measured gap in lit surface and in moving surface is 0.08 to 0.40 percentage points, against a stated tolerance of 3. A body's brightening of the room around it agrees to within 6%. A gap approaching the tolerance is a finding, not a reason to move the tolerance.
- **WebGPU does not throw when a frame is invalid.** `submit` silently drops a command buffer that fails validation, the canvas keeps whatever it had, and the wall shows a frozen frame — which in a room lit only by the particle field is indistinguishable from a quiet gallery, the same indistinguishability `renderer-health.ts` exists for one layer up. The renderer takes an `onError` callback fed from the device's `uncapturederror` event, and the regression suite fails on anything it collects.
- **`layout: 'auto'` gives every pipeline its own bind group layout.** This was found the expensive way: the room and paper pipelines differ only in blend state, shared a bind group built from one of them, and every paper frame was dropped. The symptom was a static black wall with no error anywhere. Both render pipelines take an explicit `GPUPipelineLayout` now, which is also the documentation of what the bindings are.
- **A body must brighten the room where the body is.** Aggregate checks — how much of the surface is lit, how much of it moved — cannot see a vertical flip, and the two APIs hand pixels back in opposite row order (`readPixels` from the bottom, a 2D canvas from the top). The suite compares luminance in a window of the *room*, not of the image, and takes the row direction as an argument.
- **Capability is probed, never inferred.** `assessWebGpu` reads declared adapter limits and returns either a population that is a whole number of workgroups or a refusal naming the limit with its number. Rounding the population *up* to a whole workgroup would be harmless on the GPU and dishonest in the console, which would then report a particle count the field does not contain.
- **The WGSL is a port, and a constant tuned on one backend only is a silent change to what the piece looks like.** `webgpu-shaders.test.ts` reads both shader sources and checks that every number carrying an artistic rule appears in both — the memory curl scale that stopped the swarm collapsing onto filaments, the inner radius that stopped a visitor punching a dark hole in the field, and twenty-odd others. Neither backend is mutation-tested, for the reason `swarm-renderer.ts` already was not: a Node runner cannot observe a mutant inside a shader. What is mutated is the part that decides rather than draws — `room-renderer.ts` and `webgpu-capability.ts`.
- **A canvas that has held a WebGPU context cannot later hold a WebGL one.** This was written here as a note and left as "the caller's problem"; the caller never solved it, and the measurement below shows the statement was also weaker than the behaviour. See *The canvas is a resource with a lifetime*.

## Selecting a backend, and telling the operator which one they got

- **The reason belongs on the line that persists, not in the rolling event field.** Falling back from WebGPU to WebGL wrote its explanation to `lastEvent`, and the next thing the room noticed overwrote it about a second later — the integration test caught it by reading the console after warm-up and finding a renderer line that said *what* was running and nothing about *why*. An operator asks that question an hour later, not a second later. `RendererStartup.label` carries the reason whenever the backend is not the one that was selected, and `degraded` is a value rather than something the component infers.
- **`auto` cannot be disappointed; an explicit selection can.** That is the whole of the `degraded` rule, and deriving it from "the chosen backend is not the requested one" was wrong in the case that matters: the policy downgrades *before* anything is attempted, so a fallback decided by `chooseRendererBackend` looked exactly like a successful `auto` start.
- **Two settings that do the same thing is a trap.** `rendererBackend` deliberately has no `traces` option: choosing the line renderer over the particle field is what `VisualEngine` is for, and an operator with one of them set the way they want and the other one winning has no way to tell which. The preference picks the GPU API; `traces` is only ever an outcome.
- **A failed WebGPU attempt must leave the room a renderer.** `WebGpuRoomRenderer.create` still refuses as early as it can — no adapter, limits too small, a shader the driver rejects — all before it asks the canvas for anything, because a refusal that costs nothing beats one that costs an element. It is no longer what makes the fallback work: `startRoomRenderer` takes a `RenderSurface` and a fresh element per construction, so the paths that ordering could never cover are covered.
- **An async renderer needs a retirement flag.** The effect can be torn down while an adapter is being acquired, and a renderer nobody is holding is a leaked GPU device on a machine that runs for weeks. The promise checks whether it was retired and destroys what it built.

## What the supervisor is told, and why it is not the launcher's heartbeat

- **The launcher reads rates; `roomd` reads counters, and that is the distinction, not duplication.** A rate can be reported by a loop that has stopped, if whoever computes it is not the loop — which is why the existing heartbeat measures frames against the *watcher's* clock. Counters cannot pretend at all: `roomd` holds the arrival time itself and watches `engineTick`, `renderFrame` and `sensorFrame` separately, so a dead page, a frozen renderer, a stalled engine and an unplugged camera are four findings rather than one. Only the first three restart anything; a camera that has been unplugged is a degraded artwork, and a supervisor that restarts the browser over it produces a restart loop in front of visitors.
- **The engine tick is the analysis loop past its clock gate, and the sensor frame is a camera frame actually analysed.** They are genuinely different counters: demo mode, simulation and replay all advance the world model with no camera contributing, which is exactly the case `sensorStalled` exists to describe without acting on.
- **The supervisor beat goes to loopback only.** The standards already record that the existing heartbeat is the one respect in which the published copy is not offline. A second report to a CDN edge would be a second exception for no benefit, since nothing out there supervises anything, so `isSupervised` gates it on the hostname.
- **`deny_unknown_fields` makes the wire format a two-sided contract with teeth.** A counter added in TypeScript and not in Rust does not degrade the report, it stops the supervisor seeing the room at all. `supervisor-beat.test.ts` reads the Rust struct and compares field sets; `the_beat_the_browser_sends_is_the_beat_this_reads` parses the literal JSON the browser produces. Verified by hand as well: 204 for the real beat, 400 for the same beat with one extra field.
- **Run the release gates one at a time.** `npm run test:mutation` forks a runner per mutant, `npm run test:render` launches headless Chrome on the GPU, and a release `cargo build` takes every core. All three at once took this machine down hard enough to need a restart. `npm run check` sequences them; do not parallelise it by hand.

## Porting the room's interest, and what nearly went wrong

- **A spell that has just begun reports nobody, and that is the contract.** `focus()` computes the attack curve and returns null for a weight of zero, so the exact frame on which a visitor crosses the threshold still says the room is attending to no one. It reads like an off-by-one and it is the point — interest that switches on reads as a trigger. Four of the first Rust tests asserted the opposite and were wrong, not the port. `decodeAttentionFrame` refuses a frame that claims to be attending with a weight of zero, so a shell that latches on "attending" cannot quietly turn the ramp into a step.
- **A revisit plus a crossing is 0.92, and the threshold is 1.** The two strongest things that are not dwelling do not, together, earn the room's interest. Every test that reaches for "two strong acts" has to pick which two; three of them originally picked this pair and silently exercised the *uninterested* path while claiming to exercise a spell.
- **Insertion order is load-bearing, and a `HashMap` would hide that for months.** Evidence lives in a JS `Map`, the candidate search takes the first track to reach the highest total, and re-`set`ting an existing key does not move it. The Rust holds a `Vec` of pairs for that reason alone. Any hash-ordered container passes every test with one candidate and diverges the first time two visitors qualify on the same frame — which is a crowd, which is a Saturday.
- **The bound is derived from four cooldowns in three files.** Inside the 90 s evidence window: dwell and stillness are one each per 20 s gate, so five each; `behaviors.ts` cools a revisit 40 s, so three; `memory-disturbance.ts` cools a crossing 240 s, so one. Fourteen, against `FILINGS_PER_TRACK_MAX` of 32. The test drives every source at exactly its own cooldown and asserts the measured peak against that derivation rather than against the cap, so the cap can never start changing behaviour unnoticed. The first version of that test drove crossings at 10 s — a rate the room cannot produce — and reported 22, which is the instrument being wrong rather than the bound.
- **The boundary refuses the two kinds the gate owns.** `Dwell` and `Stillness` have wire codes because the enum has four variants, and `AttentionEngine::step` rejects a frame that files either. A caller filing them directly has bypassed the 20 s spacing that is the whole reason `EvidenceGate` exists, and the room would become interested in the first person who stops walking — the measured failure that gate was added for.
- **Rust is not faster here either, by more than the last two subsystems were not.** 0.00023 ms a frame in WASM against 0.00030 ms in TypeScript, over 73,360 frames, including every copy across the boundary. That is a thousandth of one percent of a frame in both directions. The reason to have it is the same as before: a deterministic core that replays without a browser.
- **This leg agrees exactly, and that is worth stating rather than rounding to "within tolerance".** The interest rules are comparisons, one subtraction and one division — no `hypot`, no `powf`, nothing that reaches `libm` — so TypeScript, native and `wasm32` produce bit-identical weights over the whole run. The stated tolerance is 1e-9 and the measured error is 0. A future run that reports anything non-zero has found a real change, not floating-point weather.
- **A parity run built entirely out of quiet rooms proves nothing.** Interest is rare on purpose, so eight scenarios and seventy thousand frames can agree perfectly while never once reaching the decision under test. The report carries a `spells` count and the gate fails below eight.


## Live WASM tracking migration

- **Track spelling is part of failover continuity.** Rust returns `t-<sequence>`;
  TypeScript returns `visitor-<birth in base 36>-<sequence>`. The standalone
  parity harness compared tracking properties without exposing this integration
  difference. `TrackingRuntime` restores the shell's spelling before comparing
  or emitting Rust frames; otherwise switching engines silently loses attention
  and behaviour history attached to those IDs. The live adapter now runs against
  real WASM inside the tracking parity gate, including reset.
- **A fallback needs the history that failed.** Starting a fresh TypeScript
  tracker after a WASM trap would forget everyone and split every path. Opt-in
  WASM tracking keeps a live TypeScript companion, never an unbounded replay log,
  and retires WASM on a disagreement or failure. This temporary duplication is
  for continuity, not speed. Loading is bounded and late or unmounted WASM
  instances are freed; diagnostics keep only a fixed reason, never thrown data.

## Live WASM interest, and the read model it forced

- **A curve sampled at will cannot agree with an engine across a boundary.** `RoomAttention.focus(now)` recomputed the attack/hold/fade weight at whatever instant a caller passed, while the Rust engine decides once per frame and hands back a number. Comparing this instant against the last frame would have retired WASM on the difference every time. The room now decides on a sensing frame and `decision()` reports what it decided; `depositScale` and `favours` take no clock at all.
- **The size of that change is bounded and was measured rather than waved at.** Within one sensing frame the weight moves by at most `dt / INTEREST_ATTACK_MS`, which at 24 Hz is 0.0105, and it moves at all only during the four-second attack and the fifteen-second fade — through the forty-five-second hold it is exactly 1 either way. The largest effect on the artwork is a deposit multiplier of at most 1.45 being at most 0.0047 out, for one visitor, for a few seconds, a few times a day. It is also the model the engine should have had: coupling correctness to when a caller happens to ask is what an explicit `dt` exists to avoid.
- **Half a subsystem on each side of a boundary is not a migration.** In Rust the twenty-second evidence spacing lives *inside* `AttentionEngine`, so a shell keeping its own `EvidenceGate` and filing dwell directly would have had the two halves disagreeing about which frame a filing landed on. `AttentionRuntime` absorbs the gate, and the boundary refuses `Dwell` and `Stillness` outright — a caller filing those has bypassed the spacing the gate exists for.
- **Do not bracket a tolerance against a copy of it.** The parity test offset a weight by `2**-20` and `2**-19` and then asserted `2**-20 < 1e-6 && 2**-19 > 1e-6` — two literals the compiler folds to `true`, which the linter caught as constant truthiness and which is exactly the "assert properties, not tautologies" rule being broken. Change the tolerance and the brackets silently stop straddling it while the test keeps passing. `INTEREST_PARITY_TOLERANCE` is exported and the test reads it.

## Live WASM memory field, and the batch it is sent in

- **The field is not like the other two, because callers hold it.** Tracking and interest hand back small records; the field is 2,304 cells the swarm uploads as a texture and `memoryDensity` reads to choose the room's personality. So the companion's `Float32Array` *is* the field, handed out once and never reassigned — not on a reset, not on a fallback, not when an engine attaches mid-session. Swapping in a second runtime when WASM arrived would leave the renderer pointed at an array nothing updates, which on the wall is a memory that stopped moving.
- **An engine attaches to a running field rather than starting one.** The room draws from its first frame and only later finishes loading WASM, so an engine that started empty would disagree on its first comparison. `attach` adopts whatever the room already remembers.
- **Effects come back as values, which is how a second engine can apply them.** `upkeep` still decays the field in place — it is a typed array the renderer holds — but it now returns a `FieldDecay[]` of exactly what it applied, and `FieldRuntime.decayed` replays that into Rust. Re-deriving `forgettingRate` and the overnight rule at the call site would have put half of `upkeep` somewhere else, where the two copies could drift.
- **Only the last snapshot of a turn is comparable.** The companion has applied every decay by the time the runtime is told; an intermediate snapshot is the engine partway through a sequence that has already finished. Checking each one reported a divergence on the one turn that decays twice — the night — which is the turn nobody is awake to see was wrong.
- **A past-tense name is load-bearing.** `decayed(decays)` means the caller has already moved the field and the engine is being caught up; `decay(...)` does both. The first version was called `advance` and the first test written against it was wrong in exactly the way the name invited.
- **The deposit rule is read, never restated.** `observedDeposits` returns what the observed visitors owe as values, and both the field and the runtime consume it. Two copies of "a still visitor deposits 0.0018 over a radius of 3.4" is two copies that can drift, and the drift would surface as a parity failure blamed on Rust.

### What the parity harness could not see until it was broken on purpose

- **A reversed deposit/decay order is only visible at a steep decay.** At the room's real rate — one second against a 120-minute half-life — the two orders differ by about 1.7e-7 in a step, under the 1e-6 tolerance. The twelve-hour leg still catches a reversed adapter, because the field is stateful and that error compounds until it crosses; but it reports it as a fallback after 43,200 steps and names no rule. A one-minute half-life over thirty seconds separates the orders by 0.146, so the added segment fails at once and says which rule broke.
- **A double free in a `finally` buried the assertion that had just failed.** `FieldRuntime` owns every engine it is handed and frees it on retire, so freeing one again in cleanup threw a null pointer out of `WasmFieldEngine.free` and the harness printed a wasm-bindgen stack for what was actually a parity defect. The first reading of that output was "the harness is blind to ordering", which was wrong — the harness was fine and its cleanup was not. Dispose what owns the engine; never free it twice.
- **That mistake is the standing rule in miniature.** Validate the instrument before theorising about the measurement — and when the instrument looks blind, check that it is not simply being shouted over.

## The canvas is a resource with a lifetime

- **A canvas element is consumed by the first graphics API it is asked for, and nothing gives it back.** Measured in this machine's Chrome rather than read from a specification, because the specification is weaker than the behaviour and two of the five results were stronger than what this file already claimed:

  | what was done to a canvas | can it then hold a WebGL2 context |
  | --- | --- |
  | nothing | yes |
  | `getContext('webgpu')` and configured | no |
  | configured, then `unconfigure()` and `device.destroy()` | **no** |
  | `getContext('webgpu')` alone, never configured | **no** |
  | `getContext('webgl2')`, then asked for `'webgpu'` | no, in that direction too |

  There is no cleanup path, *asking* is enough, and the exclusivity runs both ways. So this is a property of canvases, not a WebGPU quirk to be routed around, and `app/globals.css` already knew half of it: the particle field and the trace renderer have separate elements because one canvas cannot hold both a WebGL2 and a 2D context. This is the same rule over time rather than at one instant.
- **Three defects that look unrelated were all one canvas element living for the life of the page.** The backend was fixed by whichever API was asked for first, and nothing ever replaced the element:
  - An operator changing **Graphics backend** in the console got a fallback and a truthful sentence about a canvas, forever. The room runs WebGL2 by default, so the element had already been spent before anyone opened the console, and the setting appeared to work while only ever taking effect on a reload.
  - A WebGPU device loss — a projector renegotiating, a driver resetting, the ordinary event this installation is built to survive — rebuilt onto the same element. If the adapter did not come back, WebGL2 was refused by the *canvas* rather than by the machine, and the room dropped to Canvas 2D traces for the rest of the gallery day on hardware that could have run the field.
  - A WebGPU attempt that failed *after* `getContext` left the fallback building on an element it had already spent. Ordering inside `create` was the only thing holding this shut, and ordering cannot cover `configure` throwing.
- **All three are silent**, which is why none of them had been found. This room is dark by design and lit only by the field, so a thinner renderer looks like a quieter gallery — the same indistinguishability `renderer-health.ts` and `diagnoseSensing` exist for one layer up, one more layer up again.
- **The rule is now code.** A renderer is never handed an element; it is handed a `RenderSurface` and takes a `fresh()` one immediately before every construction it attempts. `GpuSurface` owns the elements and the render tree owns only a positioned wrapper, because a canvas here is a resource with a lifetime rather than markup. A spent element is detached so its drawing buffer can be collected, and the line renderer — which uses no canvas — releases the surface rather than leaving one mounted.
- **`renderer-mount.ts` exists because the recovery was reachable only by losing a real GPU context in a real browser.** It is the same shape as `mountTracking` and `mountAttention`: one call, one cleanup. Moving it out of the effect took the render component from 1,249 lines to 1,193, and the ceiling ratcheted with it.
- **Validate the instrument, then break it.** The measurement above came first, and the tests written from it were then checked by breaking the fix: hoisting `surface.fresh()` out of `startRoomRenderer` fails four startup tests, and dropping `surface.release()` from the mount cleanup fails two mount tests. The first version of the rebuild test gave each mount its own host and asserted the two canvases differed — which is true by construction and would have passed against the unfixed code. A test for "a fresh element" has to share the host the render tree shares.
